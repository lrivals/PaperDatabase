## Abstract

The perception of drones, also known as Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs), particularly in infrared videos, is crucial for effective anti-UAV tasks. However, existing datasets for UAV tracking have limitations in terms of target size and attribute distribution characteristics, which do not fully represent complex realistic scenes. To address this issue, we introduce a generalized infrared UAV tracking benchmark called Anti-UAV410. The benchmark comprises a total of 410 videos with over 438 K manually annotated bounding boxes. To tackle the challenges of UAV tracking in complex environments, we propose a novel method called Siamese drone tracker (SiamDT). SiamDT incorporates a dual-semantic feature extraction mechanism that explicitly models targets in dynamic background clutter, enabling effective tracking of small UAVs. The SiamDT method consists of three key steps: Dual-Semantic RPN Proposals (DS-RPN), Versatile R-CNN (VR-CNN), and Background Distractors Suppression. ## I. INTRODUCTION

W ITH the rapid advancements in deep learning and au- tomation, Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) have become ubiquitous and widely used in various fields such as aerial photography and environmental monitoring [1]. However, along with their widespread adoption, UAVs also pose potential threats to public safety. Therefore, it is crucial to monitor their operations, including their locations and trajectories, in order to ensure safety and security. However, due to the small size of UAVs and the complexity of their surrounding environments, locating and tracking UAVs remains a challenging problem that requires careful consideration.

Traditional datasets for thermal infrared object tracking [2], [3], [4], [5], [6] primarily emphasize tracking of large-scale targets, which may not be ideal for small object tracking scenarios such as UAVs. Although the Anti-UAV dataset [2] has been introduced to address UAV tracking, it still exhibits limitations in terms of realistic scene representations. The drones in this dataset are often susceptible to easy observation due to their large size and clean backgrounds, which do not fully capture the complexity of real-world tracking situations.

In order to address this limitation, we have developed a new dataset called Anti-UAV410, which is currently the largest benchmark for single object tracking of UAVs in the thermal infrared (TIR) domain. This dataset comprises 410 sequences with over 438 k bounding boxes in total, and is specifically designed to tackle UAV tracking challenges. Anti-UAV410 focuses on restoring the anti-UAV tracking problem in real scenarios, i.e., small UAV object tracking under long-range imaging. As shown in Fig. 1 (left), Anti-UAV410 covers various tracking scenes in the wild, such as forests, mountains, lakes, which may introduce excessive background noises and make the tracker learn from the background region. Thereby Anti-UAV410 gives rise to new challenges in tracking UAV in the wild, i.e. tiny targets and dynamic backgrounds with clutter (DBC). As a solution to the challenges posed by tiny object tracking against dynamic and ambiguous backgrounds, we propose a customized multi-branched tracking architecture called SiamDT (Siamese Drone Tracker). Due to the missing color features in thermal infrared (TIR) images, conventional TIR tracking methods often utilize shallow features such as contours for target representation [7], [8], [9], [10], [11], which are highly interpretable. However, given that UAVs are typically small in size and operate in cluttered environments, these shallow hand-crafted features are insufficient for accurately distinguishing UAVs from background distractors with similar visual appearances.

To bridge these gaps, we design a novel dual-semantic extracting mechanism to capture semantic saliency of the target that are discriminative and easily localized in the search template. The dual-semantic extracting mechanism consists of twostages modules: i) Dual-Semantic RPN Proposals (DS-RPN) that predicts candidate proposals by constructing a Siamesebranched RPN that models relations between the target region and template as well as the probability of the existence of foreground object; ii) Versatile R-CNN (VR-CNN) that refines the prediction proposals based on the fused information through shared weights' R-CNNs. Different from traditional Siamese trackers [12], [13], [14] that only learn the matching semantic between template and target candidate regions during training, SiamDT also learns an individual foreground probability semantic to determine the probability of the current target candidate region containing a foreground object. Specifically, we introduce an additional branch to output the probability of foreground semantic in the current target region, which is nearly zero when the target candidate regions have no semantic object. Based on the robust feature representation, we further introduce the third branch iii) Background Distractors Suppression that enhances trackers' discriminative capacity against dynamic background distractors by a hand-crafted map that stores all possible negative samples. Through joint action of step 1 and step 2, an inductive representation with explicit semantics about the small targets can be obtained in a superior way. Consequently, our SiamDT copes with two main challenges during tracking UAVs in realistic-like scenes, undersize target problem and blurred background problem.

Extensive experimentation was conducted to analyze the performance of state-of-the-art trackers in addressing various challenges. In summary, this work contributes in the following ways:

r We introduce a large-scale Thermal Infrared (TIR) bench- mark called Anti-UAV410, which serves as a valuable resource for anti-UAV systems and tracking methods. Furthermore, we assess the performance of existing state-ofthe-art trackers on Anti-UAV410 and provide comprehensive baselines for future comparisons.

r We propose a novel Siamese drone tracker, named SiamDT, which leverages powerful dual-semantic features to enhance discrimination of tiny targets and includes a background suppression branch to mitigate dynamic background clutter. SiamDT presents a novel scheme for TIR tracking of small targets.

r We achieve new state-of-the-art performance in tracking on the newly proposed Anti-UAV410 benchmark as well as the Anti-UAV [2] dataset, and demonstrate the generalization ability on other generic datasets such as LaSOT [15] and GOT-10k [16].

## II. RELATED WORK

### A. Anti-UAV Tracking

UAV tracking tasks can be divided into two categories: one is aerial viewpoint tracking and the other is anti-UAV tracking. Although they are both associated with drones, there is a fundamental difference between these two tracking tasks.

The aerial viewpoint tracking employs UAVs as the collecting devices and finds the region of interest from an air-to-ground perspective, with no limitations on the tracking targets. The aerial tracking task has gained a great deal of attention and achievements in recent years. Dronescapes [17] presents a large-scale UAV video dataset from real scenarios for multitask learning. UAV123 [18] and UAVDT [19] construct UAV benchmarks focusing on tracking objects in videos captured by a UAV platform. UAVDark135 [20], DarkTrack2021 [21] and NAT2021 [22] are the pioneer datasets for nighttime aerial tracking, and an associated Transformer based UAV nighttime tracker is proposed in [23].

The anti-UAV tracking, on the other hand, fixes the tracked target as a drone to simulate a real anti-UAV scenario, aiming at solving the realistic problem of tracking drones. Therefore, aerial viewpoint tracking and anti-UAV tracking are essentially two distinct tracking tasks, which are applicable in different tracking scenarios. Jiang et al. [2] proposed a multi-modal anti-UAV dataset, consisting of 318 pairs of TIR-visible video sequences, providing valuable resources for anti-UAV tracking research. Nevertheless, the targets in the dataset are mostly large-sized, and the tracking scenes mainly comprise skies or urban buildings, which may not be as complex as real-world scenarios. Zhao et al. [24] proposed the DUT dataset, which contains 10,000 visible UAV images for the detection task and 20 visible sequences for the tracking task. Unfortunately, the amount of tracking portion of the DUT dataset is far less to be used for training and evaluation of anti-UAV algorithms. To overcome the limitations methond above, we propose the Anti-UAV410 dataset, which includes more realistic scenes to better simulate actual tracking scenarios.

### B. Thermal Infrared Datasets

Early thermal infrared (TIR) datasets have primarily been used for pedestrian detection [25], [26]. In recent years, with the advancements in TIR sensors, several TIR datasets have been proposed, which have contributed to the development of object tracking. Berg et al. [27] introduced the first TIR benchmark (VOT-TIR2015) for tracking, consisting of 20 thermal image sequences with different objects, collected from seven different sources using eight different types of sensors. VOT-TIR2016 [5] extends VOT-TIR2015 by opening a call for contributions and replacing the simple sequences with community-provided sequences. VOT-RGBT2019 [4] is a dual-mode datasets, containing total 234 sequences with an average length of 335 frames, which aims to make full use of RGB and thermal image information to enhance the tracker's performace. Liu et al. [6] proposed a large-scale dataset (LSOTB-TIR) with a wider range of objects and scenarios, posing greater challenges for tracking. LSOTB-TIR consists of 1,400 TIR sequences with more than 600 K frames, which is the largest TIR object tracking dataset to date. Zhang et al. [28] proposed using Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) to generate synthetic TIR data from abundant labeled RGB data, addressing the issue of limited TIR training samples.

However, there is a scarcity of datasets specifically designed for UAV tracking, which hinders the development of anti-UAV tasks. Recently, Jiang et al. [2] proposed a multi-modal UAV dataset, but both of the target size and the target's background are deviating from the the actual tracking scenario. To bridge the above gap, we propose the Anti-UAV410 dataset, offering a platform for training and evaluation of anti-UAV tracking algorithms.

### C. Thermal Infrared Trackers

Feature extraction, which captures fine-grained semantic information, plays a crucial role in object perception. Several methods have been proposed in the literature for feature extraction in TIR tracking. Yu et al. [29] modified the Histogram of Oriented Gradient (HOG) features based on the profile information of TIR targets and trained the tracker with dense samples, achieving promising performance in TIR tracking. Zhang et al. [7] trained a Siamese network to extract TIR features and demonstrated the effectiveness of shallow features of infrared targets in differentiating trackers. Huang et al. [8] introduced a spatio-temporal attention constraint to suppress the interference of cluttered backgrounds, thereby enhancing the tracker's ability to cope with tracking texture-less infrared targets in cluttered backgrounds. Chen et al. [30], [31] extracted handcrafted features such as motion and edge features to enhance the discriminative ability of TIR trackers. Burceanu et al. [32] proposed a dual-pathway tracking algorithm, which used one pathway to resolve noise interference and the other pathway to accommodate changes in target appearance, which provides a good idea for tracking infrared UAV targets with severe noise interference. Zhang et al. [33] provides a small target dataset (TSFMO) as well as a dedicated small target tracker (S-KeepTrack). Although S-keepTrack is designed for RGB video sequences, it can handle the challenges of small targets and fast movements, and thus it has application potential for tracking anti-drone in infrared images. Jiang et al. [2] proposed a novel training strategy that helps the tracker learn more robust class-level semantic information and obtain more discriminative instance-level features for TIR tracking. However, most of these methods primarily focus on large-sized TIR targets, and little attention has been paid to tiny target tracking. To address this issue, we develop an efficient feature extraction method and a background distractors suppression strategy for TIR tiny target tracking. These techniques aim to capture fine-grained semantic information and suppress the interference from cluttered backgrounds, enabling more accurate and robust tracking of small-sized targets in TIR images.

## III. ANTI-UAV410 BENCHMARK

Traditional datasets typically prioritize tracking of large-sized objects against clean backgrounds, which may not fully capture the real-world scenarios of drones that often operate in cluttered backgrounds as small objects. To address this limitation, we propose Anti-UAV410, a dataset that is designed to be more representative of realistic scenes, featuring numerous tracking scenes with tiny targets and dynamic backgrounds.

### A. Data Collection

To create a realistic representation of the challenges of anti-UAV tracking in real-world environments, we captured tracking videos in diverse and complex scenarios. These scenarios included two different lighting conditions (day and night), two seasons (autumn and winter), and a wide range of backgrounds such as buildings (30%), mountains (20%), forests (5%), urban areas (30%), clouds (10%), water surfaces (3%), and others. The acquired video sequences were recorded at a frame rate of 25 frames per second (FPS) in the mid-wave infrared spectrum. From these sequences, we selected 100 minutes of video for meticulous frame-by-frame annotation, totaling over 150,000 frames.

To further augment the size of the dataset, we also incorporated videos from the 1st Anti-UAV Challenge website [34] and the Anti-UAV dataset [2]. These videos were cropped and cleaned to ensure consistency and quality, resulting in the creation of the Anti-UAV410 dataset. i) Since some video sequences have text on their borders, we adopted the crop operation to remove these distractions. ii) Because Anti-UAV is a dual-modal dataset, some of the video fragments are not suitable for individual TIR tracking (e.g., some of the TIR video has no target in the first frame, while the corresponding visible video does). We discard such frames so that it can be used for independent TIR tracking. We call this process the "clean" operation. The average length of the videos in the Anti-UAV410 dataset is 1069 frames, which poses a challenge for trackers to have robust target re-detection capabilities.

### B. Annotation

Following the annotation process described in [2], we initially manually annotated the attributes of each sequence, including the size of UAVs and the presence of any UAVs. Subsequently, we annotated the UAV targets using external rectangle bounding boxes as the ground truth. The coordinates of the top-left corner, width, and height of the bounding box were recorded to represent the ground truth of the target. In cases where there was no target present in a frame, the ground truth was represented as empty.

### C. Statistics

Dataset Splitting: The Anti-UAV410 dataset is divided into three sets: the train set, which consists of 200 sequences; the validation set, which consists of 90 sequences; and the test set, which consists of 120 sequences. Unlike previous datasets, we have carefully divided the dataset into three sets based on the attributes of the sequences. The attribute distributions of the train, test and validation sets, are as shown in the Fig. 2, the three subsets are relatively evenly proportioned on each challenge attribute, and at this point using the training set can better learn the challenges faced by drone tracking in the wild scenarios. No special technical, we make the Anti-UAV410 dataset exhibit such distributions by manually adjusting.

Furthermore, the training set and validation set are derived from non-overlapping clips of the same sequence, while the test set is entirely independent, ensuring a rigorous evaluation of tracker performance.

Scale distribution: In order to replicate the challenges of anti-UAV tracking in realistic scenarios, we have focused on increasing the proportion of small targets in our Anti-UAV410 dataset. The frame size of all sequences in Anti-UAV410 is set at 640×512, and we define the target size as the diagonal length of the bounding boxes. To compare the target size distribution with other widely used TIR datasets such as PTB-TIR [26], LSOTB-TIR [6], VOT-TIR2015 [35], VOT-TIR2016 [5], Anti-UAV [2], and VOT-RGBTIR2019 [4], we have analyzed the distribution in terms of target size. Unlike other tracking tasks, there are rarely UAV targets with scales larger than 70 × 70 pixels in anti-UAV tracking missions. Given the inherently small scale of drones, we define the tiny-size UAV target more strictly. We first set the size divider for tiny and small targets to 10 pixels.

To distribute the different scale attributes as evenly as possible, we then define the size range into 4 intervals: tiny [2, 10), small [10,30), medium [30,50) and normal [50, inf]. The comparison results are presented in Table II. Notably, the Anti-UAV410 dataset exhibits a significant proportion of small targets, with more than half of the targets having sizes less than 50 pixels, and even includes a certain percentage of tiny targets (size less than 10 pixels), which are missed by other datasets such as VOT-TIR2015 [35], VOT-TIR2016 [5], PTB-TIR [26], and LSOTB [6].

Attribute Definition: To comprehensively evaluate the performance of the trackers, we have labeled each sequence in our dataset with six attributes, namely Out-of-View (OV), Occlusion (OC), Thermal Crossover (TC), Fast Motion (FM), Scale Variation (SV), and Dynamic Background Clutter (DBC), as shown in Table I. The Anti-UAV410 dataset contains a significant number of thermal crossover situations, which are commonly encountered in TIR sequences. Additionally, due to the motion characteristics of UAVs, our dataset includes considerable fast movements, making the tracking task more challenging. Furthermore, Anti-UAV410 covers a substantial amount of dynamic background clutter, which is often present in real-world scenarios. It is noteworthy that the target sizes in Anti-UAV410 are predominantly tiny, small, and medium sizes, which are significantly smaller than those in other datasets, thereby posing a formidable challenge for trackers.

### D. Compared With Anti-UAV Dataset

The Anti-UAV dataset [2] is a multi-modal dataset that includes 318 video pairs. This dataset is designed to address the challenge of anti-UAV tracking in different modalities, such as low-light conditions where vision-based trackers may perform poorly. However, our proposed Anti-UAV410 dataset is specifically tailored to address the long-range anti-UAV tracking problem. It aims to improve the tracker's robustness against background clutter and enhance its ability to discriminate small-sized UAV targets. Unlike the Anti-UAV dataset, which has relatively large target sizes (with an average diagonal length of 40 pixels) and homogeneous backgrounds (consisting mainly of sky and urban buildings), we have collected a substantial amount of wild anti-UAV tracking videos to replicate real-world scenarios with diverse backgrounds and challenging tracking conditions.

## IV. SIAMDT TRACKER

### A. Overview

Drawing inspiration from the success of Siamese trackers such as SiamATL [36], SiamFC [37], SiamRPN [13], DaSi-amRPN [14], SiamBAN [38], and SiamCAR [39], we have developed our own Siamese drone tracker (SiamDT) using a multi-branched architecture, as depicted in Fig. 3. Specifically, SiamDT employs a Siamese feature extraction subnetwork that incorporates attention mechanisms and adopts the Swin Transformer [40] as the backbone feature extractor.

Distinguished from traditional Siamese trackers, the decisionmaking subnetwork of SiamDT consists of three specialized branches. One branch is designed to suppress background noise, while another branch predicts the prior probability of the existence of semantic information targets in the target area. The last branch performs similarity evaluation between the detection image and the template. Importantly, these branches are supported by three specially designed modules: the Dual-Semantic RPN Proposals (DS-RPN), the Versatile R-CNN (VR-CNN), and the Query Convolution Module (which encodes the template to the candidate regions and predicts similarity scores through combining VR-CNN).

In the following sections, we will provide a detailed introduction to each module of the SiamDT architecture.

### B. Dual-Semantic RPN for Proposals Generation

Most one-stage Siamese trackers [13], [14], [41] rely on vanilla RPN networks to establish associations between the searching image and the template. Although these vanilla RPN networks perform well in most cases, they tend to fail or make inappropriate tracking decisions when the search image is significantly larger than the template image due to their inherent regional searching strategy. In order to overcome this limitation, we propose a novel Dual-semantic RPN (DS-RPN) network with an additional branch that performs global searching. Experimental results demonstrate that our method is capable of accurately predicting optimal candidate proposals.

Global RPN Proposals: Inspired by the approaches used in GlobalTrack [42] and Siam R-CNN [12], we incorporate an RoI pooling layer to enable global RPN generation. Specifically, in the template branch, we extract the features of the target region and process them through an RoI alignment layer. In the detection branch, the global features are extracted from the entire test image, and an RPN network is used to generate a large number of candidate proposals. Each proposal is then aligned to the same size as the target features, allowing for global matching with the template target.

### D. Three-Branch Network for Decision Making

The tracking process of our approach encompasses three branches that converge for final decision making:

Background Distractors Suppression Branch: In contrast to the conventional Siamese network framework that solely calculates the RPN proposals of the search branch, our approach performs RPN candidate proposal extraction for both branches. In the template branch, we identify distractors in the background region based on their high confidence scores. Specifically, we enforce a zero overlap rate between the bounding boxes of the distractors and the ground truth bounding boxes. Let D denote the set of background distractors, which consists of K proposals, where D = {d 1 , d 2 , . . ., d K }. We limit the number of distractors to a maximum of 50, i.e., K ≤ 50. In the search branch, multiple predictions may arise from the output of the R-CNN head due to the presence of background distractors, making it challenging to discriminate between them. Let P denote the set of N hard-to-discriminate candidate predictions, where P = {p 1 , p 2 , . . ., p N }. We calculate the correlation score of the prediction results with the background distractors using the following formula:

G(D, P ) = i R -CNNHead(Ψ corr (D, p i )),(7)

where G(D, P ) computes the similarity between the prediction candidates P and the background distractors D. We use the maximum confidence score G(D, p i ) of the R-CNN predictions as the similarity output of p i to D. A higher value of G(D, p i ) indicates a higher probability that p i is a background distractor. Hence, we leverage G(D, p i ) as a negative feedback to adjust the final prediction result. The set D is initialized with the distractors from the first frame, and the features of each background distractor are stored in a sequence memory. If we obtain a reliable tracking result at a certain frame, we update D accordingly; otherwise, D remains unchanged.

Matching Semantic Branch: Following the approach of traditional Siamese trackers [12], [42], we perform a forward pass of DS-RPN, Query Convolution Module, and VR-CNN using the template image from the first frame and the search image of the current frame as input. Subsequently, we extract the top N proposals from the output.

Foreground Probability Semantic Branch: Considering the challenge of extracting effective feature representations for tiny targets with limited pixel occupancy, we introduce a third branch for foreground probability semantics. This branch is designed to output a prior probability value ranging from 0 to 1, indicating the likelihood of the current proposal containing the semantic UAV target. Subsequently, we select the top M proposals from this branch for the final decision-making process.

Remark 1: During the final decision stage, we obtain N proposals from the matching semantic branch, each with a matching semantic score, as well as similarity scores of background distractors from the background distractors suppression branch. Additionally, the foreground probability semantic branch outputs M proposals that may contain the semantic UAV target, and calculates IoU scores between these proposals and the previous N proposals. These three scores are linearly combined, and the proposal with the highest score is selected as the final tracking result.

## V. EXPERIMENTS

We conduct a qualitative and quantitative comparison between our proposed SiamDT tracker and 25 state-of-the-art trackers on our Anti-UAV410 dataset. We also analyze in detail the performance of each tracker under different attribute challenges, and discuss their potential contributions to the advancement of the anti-UAV tracking in the wild. Furthermore, we conduct experiments on other RGB datasets to validate the generalization ability of SiamDT in RGB tracking.

### A. Experimental Settings

Implementation Details: For the Siamese feature extraction subnetwork, we utilize the Swin Transformer architecture [40] as the backbone. The hyperparameters including the size of the shifted window, query dimension of each head, and the expansion layer of each multi-layer perception (MLP) are set to 7, 32, and 4, respectively. SiamDT takes Swin-Tiny as the backbone by default, its hidden channels are set to 96, and the selected layer numbers are {2, 2, 6, 2}. We use the backbone weights pre-trained by the COCO dataset [45] and train a total of 12 epochs.

The classification loss L cls and regression loss L reg are computed using binary cross-entropy and smooth L 1 loss, respectively. Our template branch and search branch are mirrored to ensure consistency. We first feed the networks with the template image followed by the search image, and then vice versa. The total loss of this bi-directional process is used as the final complete loss.

When the VR-CNN outputs two bounding boxes bbox 1 and bbox 2 with the highest confidence scores S 1 ∈ [0, 1] and S 2 ∈ [0, 1] respectively, we update the template branch and the background distractor proposals D if the intersection over union (IoU) between bbox 1 and bbox 2 is greater than 0.8, and S 1 + S 2 > 1.9.

Evaluation Metrics: Three commonly used evaluation metrics, namely precision plot, success plot, and state accuracy, are utilized to assess the performance of all trackers through One-Pass Evaluation (OPE). Specifically, the precision plot calculates the percentage of frames in which the estimated target location falls within a given distance threshold from the ground truth. We use a threshold of 20 pixels to rank the trackers. The success plot measures the fraction of successful frames where the Intersection over Union (IoU) between the predicted bounding box and the ground truth is greater than a threshold ranging from 0 to 1. The Area Under Curve (AUC) of the success plot is used to rank the trackers. The state accuracy, as defined in Anti-UAV [2], reflects the average overlap ratio between the predicted bounding boxes and the ground truth across all sequences. This metric not only requires the tracker to accurately predict the position and scale of the target, but also to determine the state of the target's presence in the current frame, and make a judgment when the target disappears from the field of view.

### C. Ablation Studies

To verify the effectiveness of different components of our SiamDT, we conducted comprehensive ablation experiments and performed detailed analysis. Importantly, we kept the hyperparameters during training consistent to ensure a fair comparison. All experiments were conducted on the Anti-UAV410 test set and validation set. We used state accuracy (SA) to evaluate trackers, and details about the SA metric can be found in [2].

We used the following notations in our experiments: GS for global search from the whole image, MS for matching semantics of the first frame, FS for foreground probability semantics of drones, and BDS for background distractors suppression module. Additionally, we created two baseline methods, SiamMS and SiamFS. SiamMS is similar to traditional Siamese trackers that only exploit the matching semantics between the target and the template, while SiamFS is similar to detection methods that ignore spatio-temporal information of the sequence.

Effect of Global Search strategy: SiamNGS (Non Global Search, NGS) represents the same algorithm as SiamDT, but adopts the same neighborhood search strategy as SiamRPN to replace SiamDT's global search. Because UAV targets are usually very small, this results in a relatively small percentage of the neighborhood search area and the full image, and a fast-moving UAV can easily escape from such search area, rendering the tracker completely invalid.

## VI. CONCLUSION

In this paper, we have focused on the problem of anti-UAV tracking. We first proposed a new large-scale TIR dataset called Anti-UAV410, which contains a variety of actual UAV videos, to provide a benchmark for evaluating anti-UAV tracking algorithms. Furthermore, we proposed a Siamese tracking method called SiamDT that can effectively handle the challenges of tiny targets and dynamic background clutter in anti-UAV tracking scenarios. Extensive experiments conducted on Anti-UAV410 and other popular datasets demonstrated the effectiveness of our proposed method in achieving state-of-the-art performance in anti-UAV tracking.

In our future work, we plan to further enhance the dataset by adding more information about the camera, such as azimuth angle and pitch angle, to facilitate the establishment of motion models and improve the design of anti-UAV systems. This additional information can potentially enable better understanding of the UAV's motion patterns and lead to more robust tracking algorithms. We believe that our work will contribute to the development of the anti-UAV industry and pave the way for further advancements in this field.
