A SOLUTION TO THE CARBON INK TOMOGRAPHY PROBLEM XENON CONTRAST FOR CARBON-INK TOMOGRAPHY

Hannes Hoffmann¹* ¹ Independent Researcher · ORCID 0009-0001-4135-9509 * Presenting and corresponding author

Differential xenon sorption creates X-ray contrast between carbon ink and papyrus.

Xenon adds a high-Z label. As a noble gas, it interacts with the material through van der Waals forces without reacting chemically. Differences in composition, pore structure and surface chemistry become measurable differences in attenuation.

Why the ink remains invisible

Carbon ink and papyrus differ chemically and structurally. Davy’s observations of incomplete carbonization in Herculaneum papyri suggest that substantial compositional differences from carbon-based ink can persist [2]. Both materials nevertheless consist mainly of light elements and can have very similar X-ray attenuation. The writing therefore typically remains invisible in conventional X-ray tomography.

Kinetic and spectral readout

Sorption and desorption produce a characteristic time signature. Energy bins around the xenon K-edge isolate the high-Z sorbate from the low-Z substrate.

The contrast problem

Several classes of artefacts, including Herculaneum papyri and mummy cartonnage, preserve writing inside closed or layered structures. Reading this writing without opening or dismantling the objects requires tomographic imaging. Resolving the internal layers, however, is only useful for reading if the ink can also be distinguished from its support.

What the model experiment showed

Xenon increased attenuation in ink particles and more weakly highlighted

papyrus fibres. Contrast followed pressure and time; measurements below and above the Xe K-edge identified xenon spectrally. After 1 h under vacuum, xenon was no longer detectable. No visible damage was observed in the model.

Hannes Hoffmann’s outlook

Competitive, kinetic Kr–Xe displacement Preloading the sample with krypton before switching to xenon would make local Kr loss and Xe gain observable. The exchange pattern could show whether xenon replaces the weaker sorbate and how quickly the front reaches ink and papyrus. It could separate sorption selectivity from restricted transport through pore structures and allotropes.

Limits of the demonstration

The model isolates the contrast mechanism. Original artefacts vary widely in their material properties. The demonstration does not establish readable contrast in an original artefact or a practical pressure, temperature and dose window for an intact object.

Photon-counting micro-CT Compact, transportable X-ray tube setups could offer this approach at substantially lower cost and with easier access than synchrotron beamtime. Detector thresholds below and above the 34.56 keV Xe K-edge could provide a xenon-specific volume from a single polychromatic scan, reducing misregistration between sequential energy scans. This would require calibrated narrow bins, sufficient counts near the edge and micrometre-scale resolution; cost, dose and access would determine its practical value.

What the time series can encode

Static attenuation reports where xenon is present. Loading and venting additionally reveal access paths, exchange rates and retention. These observables may separate ink from papyrus even when their equilibrium xenon concentrations overlap.

¹³¹Xe neutron resonance tomography Enriched stable ¹³¹Xe has isotope-specific resonances that time-of-flight neutron transmission could identify. Resonant neutron attenuation could yield substantially greater contrast enhancement. This route could reconstruct xenon density independently of X-ray attenuation and provide a second test of sorption selectivity. A pulsed epithermal source, achievable spatial resolution and activation of heritage material require prior assessment.

¹²⁹Xe MRI with isotopic enrichment Isotopically enriched ¹²⁹Xe could strengthen the conventional MRI signal from sorbed xenon without ionizing radiation. Chemical shift and relaxation may report local sorption environments. Hyperpolarization is not assumed because it is lost upon sorption. The main challenges are the weak thermal-polarization signal and spatial resolution across thin ink layers.

Cooling-assisted xenon loading Cooling promotes xenon physisorption and can replace pressurization. Cooling and pressure can also be combined. At low xenon surface coverage, an estimate using 22–27 kJ mol ¹ ⁻ binding energies gives about 2–3× more sorption at 0 °C than at 25 °C, or a comparable loading at 0.3–0.5× the pressure.

Conclusion The model demonstrates xenon sorption as a source of ink–papyrus contrast, with spatially heterogeneous loading and unloading. This establishes a material contrast mechanism and motivates kinetic imaging of hidden writing. Measurements: BAMline, BESSY II, Helmholtz-Zentrum Berlin, 11 April 2024.
