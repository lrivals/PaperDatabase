• Reduce round-trip communication latency

Performing real-time analytics in few milliseconds is critical in latency sensitive applications such as autonomous driving systems [6] and robotics, and improves the user experience in interactive applications such as virtual reality. On the other hand, heavy reliance on a remote cloud could introduce communication latencies in the order of several hundreds of milliseconds [7].

• Lower cost:

The exponential rise in edge devices generate massive amounts of data. To process such a vast amount of data at the cloud requires substantial communication infrastructure and cloud computing resources. Instead, processing data at the edge significantly reduces this burden and lowers the overall ownership cost.

• Preserve data privacy

In applications that involve private data, such as face recognition systems and personal assistants, sending data to a remote cloud carries data privacy risks. These risks can be mitigated by ensuring that the processing takes place at the edge to minimize or eliminate transmission of private data to the cloud.

• High availability

Processing at the edge is required to handle scenarios where the device may need to operate o✏ine or to address network bandwidth issues in wide-scale deployments.

#### 1.1.1. FPGA Accelerators for Edge AI

The widely-used computational platforms for edge AI systems include multi-core Central Processing Units (CPU), Graphics Processing Units (GPU), Field Programmable Logic Arrays (FPGA), and Application-Specific Integrated Circuits (ASIC) [13]. Multi-Core CPUs are a popular choice for edge computing, due to the ease of programming, low cost, and modest power consumption. However, these systems lack sufficient parallelism and incur memory access bottlenecks that often prevent them from meeting the realtime performance. GPUs are commonly used for deploying CNNs, as the highly parallel computing architecture lends itself well to executing matrix computations of the CNNs.

However, GPUs lack power efficiency. In contrast, FPGA and ASIC technologies are generally more power-efficient and provide opportunities for application-specific low-level optimizations to achieve high performance and energy efficiency [14,15]. ASIC-based CNN accelerators have the highest performance and energy efficiency, but su↵er from high design costs and time while lacking the flexibility to accommodate post-manufacturing changes. On the other hand, FPGAs not only provide for a high degree of specialization, but also allows for design changes at any point in time.

In order to meet the real-time performance requirements and stringent power budgets, optimizations based on model quantization [16][17][18][19][20] and pruning [21][22][23][24][25][26][27][28][29] are commonly applied when deploying CNNs on edge computing systems. A variety of model quantization techniques have been proposed based on half-precision floating-point, fixed-point, and integer formats. In addition, logarithmic data representation schemes and extreme precisions such as 1/2-bit representations have been demonstrated to be e↵ective with custom training routines [16][17][18][19]. Model pruning techniques include structured pruning [21][22][23][24]29] and fine-grained sparsity based methods [25][26][27][28].

CPUs and GPUs are general-purpose devices, where the above optimizations could be applied only at the software level, which limit the e↵ectiveness of the optimizations at the hardware level. For instance in GPUs, the data-representation support is limited to a few standard formats such as 8-bit integers, single, and half-precision floating-point. In contrast, FPGA and ASIC based designs could be optimized in multiple hardware abstractions, from the top-level hardware architecture to low-level components such as processing elements, arithmetic units and registers, and even at the gate level for ASIC designs. In each level, optimization techniques based on bit-width, dataflow, parallelism, and pipelining could be applied to achieve overall higher performance-per-watt. In particular, highly optimized FPGA based CNN designs have been shown to outperform general-purpose CPU and GPU devices in terms of performance and energy efficiency [27,28,[30][31][32][33][34][35]. In comparison to ASICs, FPGAs have a higher degree of flexibility, which enable custom optimizations to be applied even after deployment. Hence, FPGAs provide an attractive platform for CNN deployment in the rapidly changing edge AI landscape.

#### 1.1.2. Continual Learning for Edge AI

Despite the significant progress in inference capability, existing edge AI systems are unable to perform model training. In a typical edge AI system, the CNN model is first trained on a cloud server before it is deployed for inference on the edge computing system, as shown in Fig. 1.1a. The deployed models are typically trained in the cloud using a sufficiently large dataset that is representative of the target domain. The reason for this is that CNN training based on the backpropagation algorithm has very high computational and memory demands in comparison to inference [36]. Furthermore, the backpropagation process requires a large training data volume and many iterations. As a result, the training process consumes a significant amount of time and energy. Due to these reasons, model training requires high-end computing devices like server-grade GPUs or the Google TPU [37], which are not feasible to be incorporated in resource-constrained edge devices operating on tight power budgets.

The inability to train CNN models on the edge prevents them from adapting to new scenarios or changes in the application environment. In many applications, during inference, the CNN model is likely to encounter new objects or new environmental conditions di↵erent from the ones present during training. The CNN models are unable to recognize or handle such novel scenarios without undergoing retraining. To enable the CNNs to learn the new scenarios, current edge AI systems need to send the newly encountered data samples to the cloud for retraining, and then retrieve the trained model for future inference as shown in Fig. 1.1a. Thus, constant communication with the cloud is necessary for edge AI systems to achieve continuous knowledge acquisition, which negates the advantages of edge computing that were discussed earlier.

To mitigate the above limitations associated with cloud-based training, edge AI systems should be capable of learning continuously on-device. However, in addition to the above challenges, existing CNN models have an inherent limitation in continuously accumulating knowledge. Prior works show that when an artificial neural network is trained incrementally with new knowledge, the network undergoes catastrophic forgetting of previous knowledge [38,39]. This could be naively avoided by performing complete model retraining with training data that combines the new and old data samples. ### 2.2. Continual Learning

Continual learning is a paradigm of machine learning models that are capable of acquiring new knowledge continuously in an efficient manner, while retaining existing knowledge.

Such capability has been a long-standing requirement in artificial intelligent agents [74].

Recently, with the rise in popularity of deep learning in intelligent agents, continual learning in AI agents has received a renewed attention. However, performing continual learning with deep learning models is challenging and is an actively researched problem [40][41][42][43].

The main issue of continual learning is the phenomenon of catastrophic forgetting, where artificial neural networks undergo the forgetting of previous knowledge when acquiring new knowledge [38,39]. A variety of techniques is proposed in the literature to tackle this problem which falls under the following three main approaches [40][41][42][43].

• Regularization based methods

Regularization is a popular technique used in machine learning to prevent the overfitting of models, by augmenting the training loss with a penalty term. This concept is applied in continual learning to penalize changes to consolidated model parameters when acquiring new knowledge to prevent forgetting [75][76][77][78][79]. As such model parameters which are important to previously acquired knowledge is preserved from changes, and less important ones are trained for new knowledge. The existing works relying on regularization mainly di↵er in how the importance of parameters to the trained knowledge is calculated. In [80], regularization based on knowledge distillation is applied to preserve model outputs corresponding to previously learned knowledge/tasks.

• Architecture based methods

The architectural modifications is another strategy proposed in continual learning works to prevent forgetting [81][82][83][83][84][85]. This includes several techniques such as dynamic architectures, which are modified during learning, and dual memory models that splits training tasks among memories.

In dynamic architecture methods, model architectures are explicitly or implicitly modified by allocating novel resources when learning new knowledge, to minimize interference to consolidated knowledge [81][82][83]. A dual memory model consists of a fast learning module that adapts quickly to new tasks, and a slow learning module that learns a generalized representation of all the tasks [83][84][85]. This concept is biologically inspired from the complementary learning systems theory in the mammalian brain, where the hippocampus is updated frequently with episodic memories, while the neocortex learns a structured knowledge representation with interplay among them [86,87].

• Replay based methods

In replay-based methods, training samples corresponding to prior tasks are either stored or generated using generative models to prevent the forgetting of prior knowledge [84,[88][89][90][91][92][93][94][95]. The storing of prior samples to prevent forgetting is commonly known as experience replay. Existing works have shown the e↵ectiveness of storing native samples [88, 90-92, 94, 95] or intermediate features for experience replay [89,93]. The generative models, prevent storing training samples and instead generate samples corresponding to prior tasks [84,92]. In both experience and generative replay, the samples belonging to prior tasks are either mixed with new training samples [88,92] or used for regularization to prevent forgetting [90,91].

Further, it is common to see hybrid methods combining the above techniques [83][84][85]. For instance in dual memory models, replay techniques are used to transfer knowledge from the fast learning module to the slow learning module.

In addition to continual learning, a variety of learning paradigms are actively researched in deep learning. The following subsections briefly explain these paradigms and their similarities and di↵erences with continual learning.

#### 2.2.2. Transfer Learning

Transfer learning is a machine learning technique where previously learned knowledge is used to facilitate learning new knowledge [96]. In deep learning, transfer learning is used to transfer representations learned from one task, for learning other related tasks [97,98].

This allows for efficient, faster adaptation. Transfer learning is also used in scenarios

where training data of the target task is scarce. In contrast to continual learning, transfer learning techniques alone do not prevent catastrophic forgetting. However, transfer learning techniques are used in continual learning models where pre-trained CNN models are employed to train incrementally learning classifiers [1,[83][84][85]89].

#### 2.2.3. Few-Shot Learning

Few-shot learning is another popular learning paradigm that deals with learning new classes from limited training samples [99,100]. The problem deals with learning to discriminate between classes, given a few samples. However, unlike continual learning, few-shot learning does not require remembering all past classes. Recently, works based on both learning settings have been proposed in the literature [101][102][103].

#### 2.2.4. Curriculum Learning

Curriculum learning defines a sequential set of tasks that are progressively difficult, to facilitate faster learning and higher accuracy [104,105]. While sequential learning of classes is similar to continual learning, curriculum learning explicitly defines an ordering of classes based on difficulty. In contrast, continual learning does not specify a task ordering, and is expected learn tasks in a random order.

#### 2.2.5. Meta-Learning

Meta-learning is referred to as learning to learn, where it learns the meta-data about past learning experiences such as hyperparameters to improve future learning [106]. The In summary, transfer learning, few-shot learning, and meta-learning, are useful in edge AI applications with limited training data availability or where higher training efficiency for a targeted task is required. Further, the implementation of such learning mechanisms on edge devices may be necessary in performance critical applications and also to avoid potential data privacy vulnerabilities associated when sharing data between edge and the cloud. However, curriculum learning is more suited for cloud implementation rather than at the edge due to the necessity of storing training data and ordering of learning tasks in the order of difficulty.

### 2.3. Hardware Acceleration of CNN training 2.3.1 End-to-end CNN training

As mentioned earlier, CNNs are conventionally trained using the backpropagation algorithm [36]. The backpropagation algorithm consists of 3 phases of computation: forward pass (FP) of training image inputs to compute the loss, backward pass (BP ) of gradients of the loss function, and weight update step (WU), which are performed in sequence as shown in Fig. 2.3. In each iteration, a mini-batch of images is passed through the network and the intermediate activations from each layer from FP and gradients of all parameters from BP are stored until WU. In comparison, during inference only the FP is performed without the need to store intermediate activations from each layer. Thus, the computational and memory demands in CNN training are significantly higher compared to inference.

To meet these computational demands, CNNs are typically trained on high-performance computing systems in the cloud. GPUs are the most common choice for CNN training at present since the massively parallel architecture lends well for the heavy matrix operations involved in CNN operations to produce high throughput. As such, the growth in GPU technologies has been a major driver to the proliferation of CNNs [107]. In recent times, several ASIC-based accelerators have emerged as an alternative to GPUs for CNN training. Among them, Google's cloud Tensor Processing Unit (TPU) is the most popular device, which is a multi-chip system that is optimized specifically for deep learning models, and thus o↵ers high performance and energy efficiency [37]. Gaudi from Habana Labs is another promising publicly available ASIC in cloud platforms [108]. To perform training efficiently, the processing is distributed across multiple devices.

The FPGA based CNN training works have been proposed in [109][110][111][112][113][114]. However, single chip solutions for training CNNs have been limited to smaller networks and has shown to su↵er from scaling to larger batch sizes [111,[113][114][115]. Thus, multi FPGA clusters are required for training deeper networks with large batch sizes [110], [109]. The work in [112] is an exception, which proposes a sparse CNN technique to fit training on to a single chip. However, the above solutions lack the energy efficiency and cost-e↵ectiveness to be deployed in edge devices. Further, design of such large scale systems are necessary due to the requirement to handle end-to-end model training.

#### 2.3.2. On-Chip Continual Learning

Typically in edge AI systems, the CNN models are initially trained on a large dataset that is representative of the target application domain. The knowledge obtained from this initial training could be utilized to train only the last layer(s) of the CNN model, when learning new classes [1,83,85,89,116,117]. In comparison to training the entire model, this strategy is more amenable for edge implementation. In the above works, the convolutional layers are kept frozen and used as a feature extractor, while the last layer(s) are incrementally trained. This provides the opportunity to create lightweight accelerators that can be deployed in edge FPGA devices, which can perform continual learning more efficiently in comparison to the end-to-end training hardware solutions discussed in subsection 2.3.1. However, very few works have explored developing such accelerators for continual learning for object classification at the edge [118][119][120].

The works [119,120] propose FPGA-based hardware accelerators for class-incremental continual learning classifiers that leverage transfer learning using a pre-trained CNN. The work in [119] is based on a classifier using the product quantization [121] in combination with a binary associative memory. The features extracted from a pre-trained CNN is quantized using product quantization, which then gets associated to their class labels using the binary associative memory. However, the total number of anchor vectors for quantization is fixed, which may be a limitation in practical scenarios. In [120], this work is extended to use a majority vote classifier with product quantization, and the hardware design is optimized for resource usage. The hardware estimates in both these works are given for a smaller number of classes and for less complex datasets (CIFAR-10) [122].

In [118], an FPGA based accelerator is proposed for training a MLP incrementally using a custom bio-inspired algorithm based on neuromodulation [123] and hebbian plasticity [124] concepts. However, the hardware estimates are only provided for low complex dataset (MNIST [125], Fashion-MNIST [126]), and the design assumes the number of classes as a prior, which may be a limitation in practical scenarios. ### 3.2. Model Background

Self-Organizing neural networks are a type of artificial neural networks, that uses an unsupervised learning technique named competitive learning, to learn a high dimensional feature space using a discrete set of features (neurons) in a topology preserving manner [130][131][132][133]. Competitive learning is a form of unsupervised learning where neurons compete for a given input, and a subset of neurons closest to the input feature vector defined by a distance function and a neighborhood function is adapted to specialize to that input.

The concept was first proposed in Self-Organizing Maps (SOM) [130], also known as Kohonen maps where a fixed sized two dimensional lattice of neurons were incrementally adapated to learn an input feature space in a topology preserving manner. Inspired by this, Neural Gas (NG) was proposed in [131], to achieve a similar objective without a predefined neural structure. This idea was extended to Growing Neural Gas, where neurons could be dynamically added periodically and connections are formed between neurons based on a Hebbian like rule, "neurons that fire together wire together" [132].

This idea was improved in Grow When Required network(GwR) by replacing the periodic neuron adding criteria with a rule that adds neurons based on network activity [133].

Recently, several works have demonstrated the use of a self-organizing neural network for continual learning for object recognition [85,116,117,134,135]. The competitive learning, where only the neurons similar to the input are adapted prevents catastrophic interference to the consolidated knowledge in the network, makes SONNs inherently suitable for continual learning, while also being efficient. This work is particularly inspired by the work in [117], which uses the GwR selforganizing neural network in combination with a CNN. In this chapter, we propose an FPGA based custom hardware architecture for a SONN based on the GwR network, which can perform class-incremental continual learning in a streaming setting when combined with a backbone CNN network as shown in Fig. 3.1. This is the first work to propose a FPGA architecture for dynamically growing SONN, where the neurons and connections evolve over time. Previously reported hardware implementations of SONN have fixed neurons and connections. The following subsection provides a detailed discussion of the SONN algorithm.

#### 3.3.3. Exploiting Parallelism

Parallelism is exploited for the vector processing operations involving neuron weights (F) in WSEL, NADD and NTRAIN. These operations have 2 kinds of parallelism: interneuron and intra-neuron parallelism, which refer to concurrent operations between neurons and within a neuron respectively. Both parallelisms are achieved in the proposed hardware architecture by unrolling inter-neuron loops (Algorithm 1, lines 3, 22) and intra-neuron loops (lines 4, 15, 23) by # 2 and # A factors respectively. The unrolled design is executed in a systolic array of processing elements (PE Matrix) of dimension # 2 ⇥ N A . Each PE is connected with adjacent PEs supporting pipelined dataflow as shown in Fig. 3.3. This allows pipeline execution of both inter-neuron and intra-neuron loops, which results in further parallelism. Each SONN neuron is organized as a Processing Column (PC). A PC contains an array of processing elements (PE), multipliers to perform computations, and a memory bank for storing weights (F) and habituation pointers (⌘) of its local neurons, as shown in Fig. 3.4. When neurons are progressively added to SONN, they are assigned a PC in a modulo round-robin manner. Thus, when the < C⌘ neuron is added, < mod # C⌘ 2 PC will store its weights (F < ) and habituation pointers (⌘ < ), and all its computations as per Algorithm 1 will be performed locally. Due to round robin assignment, the neurons are evenly distributed across the PCs, with each PC supporting up to # <0G /# 2 neurons. The inter-neuron loops are unrolled and executed in parallel by the PCs, while intra-neuron loops are unrolled and executed in parallel within each PC.

### 4.2. Model Background

The Deep SLDA model in our work uses streaming linear discriminant analysis to train the output layer of CNNs in a class-incremental manner. The concept of incremental linear discriminant analysis was originally proposed in [141], for learning from data streams. However, Deep SLDA is the first work to adopt this idea for continual learning using deep learning models. The work in [142] proposed a similar concept where the linear discriminant analysis is used for class-incremental learning in combination with a deep learning model, but in an incremental batch learning setting. In a recent survey of class-incremental continual learning algorithms, Deep SLDA model was shown to achieve the highest accuracy for highest incremental states in memory constrained settings [143].

The Deep SLDA model combines a CNN with a Streaming Linear Discriminant analysis classifier as shown in Fig. 4.1, with the CNN acting as a feature extractor and the SLDA as the classifier. The SLDA replaces the fully-connected (FC) layers of the CNN and is fed from the embedding features extracted from the last convolutional layer of the CNN. As the model learns new classes, SLDA incrementally adapts its parameters to accommodate new knowledge, while the CNN layers remain frozen. SLDA relies on the convolutional (CONV) layers of a CNN trained on a large dataset (e.g., Imagenet [11]) to act as a generic feature extractor [98]. We have used the Resnet18 CNN model pretrained on Imagenet dataset, where features extracted from the Avg Pool (with dimension ⇡ = 512) which follows the last convolutional layer of the CNN, is fed into the SLDA.

### 5.1. Introduction

In this chapter, we present a full on-chip deep continual learning pipeline on FPGA, based on the SLDA accelerator proposed in the previous chapter. The design is implemented on a Xilinx Zynq Ultrascale+ MPSoC. First, we address the performance bottleneck associated with integration of the SLDA accelerator into the pipeline. In particular, the performance bottleneck in the Zynq MPSoC based SLDA implementation is contributed by the high communication latency between the ARM processing system (PS) and programmable logic (PL). In order to overcome this bottleneck, we implemented a custom Linux device driver for memory mapping between PS-PL. Next, we integrate the SLDA accelerator with a CNN on the Xilinx Deep Learning Processor Unit (DPU) accelerator [2] to form the full continual learning pipeline. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first full continual learning system that has been demonstrated on an FPGA. Finally, we present experiments that reveal the impact of continual learning to latency and energy consumption on the FPGA system. The work in this chapter has been published in [140].

#### 5.2.1. Platform Device Interface

In Zynq SoC/MPSoCs, a custom accelerator deployed in the programmable logic (PL)

is not automatically discoverable by the host operating a Linux kernel. As such, the custom accelerator belongs to the category of platform devices considered to be residing on a virtual platform bus. In ARM based heterogeneous systems, such a device should be visible to the Linux kernel via the Device Tree structure [148], for the host to recognize and handle the device. This is done by creating a node entry in the Device Tree Source (DTS), describing the compatible drivers, I/O address ranges and interrupts of the device.

During the kernel compilation, the Device Tree Compiler (DTC) converts the device tree into a device tree binary (dtb). The binary is read by the kernel at boot time to identify the underlying hardware including the platform devices. The Zynqmmapdrv contains a platform driver interface to obtain the target accelerator I/O address information via the Linux platform interface. The driver is registered as a platform driver at the point of driver loading, via the routine platform driver register, with an of node entry carrying an identifier 'xlnx, zynqmmapdrv'. Once registered with this identifier, Linux kernel matches accelerators in the device tree carrying same identifier. If any matching entries carrying the identifier are found in the device tree binary, the kernel calls the platform interface probe of Zynqmmapdrv while providing the accelerator resource information such as I/O address regions, sizes and interrupt information.

Specifically, this information is obtained from platform interface API using the routine platform get resource. Additionally, we use this probe context to allocate a DMA memory region and to register a character device interface as discussed in detail in a following subsection.

#### 5.2.2. Character Device Interface

To allow mapping DMA and accelerator I/O memory regions to user space applications, Zynqmmapdrv registers a character driver interface. The registration creates a device file entry in the '/dev/' directory in the filesystem. This allows a host application to access Zynqmmapdrv as a file descriptor to map the DMA memory allocated by Zynqmmapdrv and the accelerator I/O memory regions to user space virtual memory, and simply access the regions as array read and writes. The character device registration is performed during platform device probe context as mentioned previously. As a character driver, Zynqmmapdrv implements file operations open, close and mmap as shown in Fig. 5.1, with mmap used for memory mapping from kernel space to user space.

#### 5.2.3. DMA Bu↵er Allocation

Zynqmmapdrv relies on Direct Memory Access (DMA) approach for transferring large chunks of data between the ARM host and the accelerator. DMA based data transfer allows the ARM host and the accelerator to share a memory region in the main memory, and facilitate the accelerator to access the region without ARM host involvement. To achieve this, Zynqmmapdrv allocates a physically contiguous and a consistent memory region using the routine dma alloc coherent during platform interface probe context [149].

The physically contiguous allocation simplifies the DMA operations from the accelerator and avoids the need to perform complex scatter/gather operations. The consistent allocation prevents caching and presents a consistent view of the memory to both the host processor and the accelerator. To allocate a larger contiguous memory region, the dma alloc coherent makes use of the Contiguous Memory Allocator (CMA) API in the Linux kernel, through which the allocation is performed in a reserved memory region named the CMA area as shown in Fig. 5.1. The dma alloc coherent returns both physical and virtual (kernel-space) addresses of the allocated region. While the physical address is passed to the accelerator, the virtual address is mapped from kernel space to user space during memory mapping.

#### 5.2.4. Memory Mapping to User Space

Zynqmmapdrv maps the accelerator I/O regions and allocated DMA bu↵ers in kernel address space to user address space. Memory mapping avoids the potential overhead of copying data between user space and kernel space memory regions for PS-PL I/O and data communication, and instead provides user space direct access to the above regions.

The memory mapping functionality is provided via the character driver interface, by the Specifically, memory mapping of DMA bu↵er is performed using dma mmap coherent function, while the I/O regions are mapped using io remap pfn range. For I/O region mapping, page caching is prevented using pgprot noncached routine prior to mapping. Xavier NX was employed. The CPU implementations were developed using C++, based on the Eigen linear algebra library [151], while a combination of cuBLAS library [147] and custom CUDA kernels were used for the GPU implementations. Specifically, for the upper triangular covariance update (Eq. 4.2), BLAS routine symmetric rank1 operation (SSYR) was relied upon. The O2 optimization level was selected for compilation. In the GPU implementation, the SLDA model parameters are stored in the GPU memory to prevent costly memory copy for each training sample. For SLDAPlasticCov, the FPGA outperforms the CPU and GPU counterparts by approximately 24 and 4 times in terms of latency, and 25 and 6 times in terms of energy efficiency respectively. Further, the FPGA design based on the proposed SLDADiagCov outperforms the CPU and GPU counterparts by approximately 3 and 12 times in terms of latency, and 3 and 15 times in terms of energy efficiency respectively. The superior performance is achieved as a result of the application-specific parallelism and pipelining in the PEs, custom memory organization for optimal data access, and the mixed-precision arithmetic units. ### Chapter 6

Conclusions and Future Directions

### 6.1. Conclusions

Continual learning is a necessity for edge AI systems in practical applications. In this thesis, we proposed FPGA based hardware accelerators and optimization strategies for continual learning in edge AI systems. To this end, we proposed two accelerators that can perform continual learning in a streaming manner from embedding features extracted from a CNN. Further, we proposed optimizations to reduce resource, latency, and power consumption. We evaluated the accelerators using popular datasets for continual learning and demonstrated that the proposed optimizations lead to low latency and high energy efficiency than CPU and GPU based counterparts. Finally, we demonstrated a full continual learning pipeline on FPGA, by combining the SLDA based accelerator with a CNN accelerator. The experimental results showed that the proposed continual learning pipeline incurs only a minimal latency and energy overhead over an inference baseline. In this chapter, we summarise the contributions in the thesis and outline the future directions.

In Chapter 3, we proposed an FPGA based accelerator for a SONN, which learns from embedding features of a CNN by dynamically growing a layer of neurons and prevents catastrophic forgetting by penalizing interference to consolidated neurons. We incorporated application specific parallelism, memory organization, approximation strategies, and scheduling into the accelerator. The proposed optimizations lead to low latency and on-chip memory usage. The implementation based on Xilinx Zynq Ultrascale+ MPSoC achieves lower latency and power consumption in comparison to CPU and GPU based counterparts.

In Chapter 4, we presented an FPGA based accelerator for an SLDA to address the scalability limitations of the SONN to learn a large number of classes. We introduced an additional variant that achieves higher efficiency with good accuracy trade-o↵. We proposed several optimization strategies based on approximation computing, optimal memory organization, and efficient resource and data reuse, that led to significant savings in latency and energy consumption. The proposed design was implemented on Xilinx Zynq Ultrascale+ MPSoC using Xilinx Vitis.

In Chapter 5, we developed a Linux device driver to remove the large latency overheads in host and SLDA accelerator communication. The experimental results demonstrated that the FPGA based design on a Xilinx Zynq Ultrascale+ MPSoC outperforms the embedded CPU and GPU based counterparts. Finally, we combined the SLDA accelerator with Xilinx DPU to form a full continual learning pipeline on the programmable logic of a Xilinx Zynq Ultrascale+ MPSoC. The experimental results showed that the proposed pipeline is capable of learning with only a negligible overhead cost of latency, and a marginal increase in energy consumption in comparison to an inference baseline.

### 6.2. Future Directions

The work undertaken in this thesis shows that continual learning can be performed in FPGA based edge AI systems with low latency and energy consumption. As next steps, we have identified several potential extensions of this work targeting higher accuracy, efficiency, and lesser supervision, as detailed in the following subsections.

#### 6.2.1. Sparsity in SLDA

In Chapter 4, we proposed SLDADiagCov to achieve accuracy-efficiency tradeo↵. As part of future work, we plan to evaluate both structured and non-structured sparsity in the full covariance computation in SLDAPlasticCov. The sparsity evaluation will be performed as a base initialization step to determine the hardware parameters, such as the PE array dimensions and L2 scratchpad memory depth. Under structured sparsity, we plan to measure the importance of each individual tile of full covariance computation using a calibration set containing a set of base classes. Subsequently, the least important tiles based on either thresholding or user constraints could be dropped to reduce on-chip storage requirements and runtime latency. The importance of tiles could be decided by the statistics of the values in the tile. To evaluate non-structured sparsity, we plan to evaluate the importance of each individual element in each tile. This information could be used to determine the maximum number of elements in a tile to be computed, which could be used to trim the processing elements for resource savings. This study is expected to lead to a more efficient hardware at a minimal loss of accuracy. Further, as part of the study, we aim to search the optimal number of base classes required to produce a solution that generalizes well to future learning.

#### 6.2.2. Replay based Training

We plan to explore the integration of an experience replay based training mechanism to fine tune the SLDA linear classifier (Chapter 4, Eq. 4.7). Representative embedding features from past tasks would be stored in a sample bu↵er similar to [89], and then used to fine tune the linear classifier, each time the classifier weights are generated from the class means and the joint covariance. The SLDA classifier assumes each class has a unimodal distribution in the embedding feature space. However, in practical scenarios due to intra-class variances, the classes are likely to have multimodal distributions, and the gradient-based fine-tuning may shift each class towards a unimodal distribution and help achieve high accuracy. A good representation from each class is required to prevent forgetting, thus a proper sampling strategy that achieves a balance of efficiency and accuracy, will be investigated. We plan to explore the benefits of accelerating this operation in the proposed SLDA accelerator in comparison to the host ARM processor.

#### 6.2.3. Semi-Supervised Learning

In practical scenarios, labeling each training sample require significant e↵ort and may not be possible especially in a streaming learning setting. Ideally, the model should either be capable of learning with few (semi-supervised) or no (unsupervised) labels. We will explore semi-supervised learning for model learning with the SLDA based model.

To achieve this capability, we will incorporate a mechanism into the SLDA model that can estimate the class of an unlabelled training sample, or detecting if it belongs to a new class. We plan to investigate di↵erent distance metrics (Euclidean, Mahalanobis) as a confidence measure to estimate the above. This idea is inspired by the work in [142], where Mahalanobis distance is used in a linear discriminant analysis classifier as a measure of detecting out-of-distribution or adversarial samples. Based on a suitable metric, the proposed accelerator in Chapter 4 will be modified to accommodate the additional computations.

#### 6.2.4. Federated Learning

In IoT applications, deep learning models are deployed in multiple devices, where each device may get exposed to di↵erent scenarios. The aggregation of knowledge acquired by individual devices into a single model would enable models to adapt to new environments faster by leveraging on the knowledge obtained from other devices. This is achieved without explicit sharing of private data of each device. This problem is referred to as federated learning in the machine learning domain [156]. Based on the SLDA model, we aim to explore this capability, where the SLDA models learned by individual edge FPGAs would be sent to a central server. The models will be aggregated and the resultant model will be sent back to the edge. We plan to compare the e↵ectiveness of consolidating either the SLDA covariance and means, or the weights and bias of the linear classifier. Further, we plan to evaluate di↵erent aggregating mechanisms with a simple baseline of weighted average based consolidation.

In edge AI systems, security is extremely important as adversaries may have physical access to these systems. Recent studies have shown edge AI systems are vulnerable to various forms of attacks such as a) side-channel attacks, b) adversarial attacks, and c) fault injection attacks [157].
