## Abstract

We present a scheduling framework for heterogeneous multiaccelerator systems executing both hard real-time and soft real-time QoS-aware DNN inference workloads. The framework combines an online reinforcement learning (RL) scheduler with a deterministic runtime safety layer. The RL scheduler dynamically generates candidate schedules by jointly selecting sub-job priorities and accelerator assignments, while runtime certification guarantees that all HRT deadlines remain satisfied independently of the learned policy. To enable runtime certification in heterogeneous precedence-constrained systems, we introduce a schedulability-preserving prefix replacement criterion based on machine-wise cuts. The proposed criterion guarantees that runtime-generated schedule prefixes preserve the existence of a feasible continuation without requiring complete finish-time dominance with respect to a feasible reference schedule. Experimental results on heterogeneous DNN accelerator platforms show that the proposed framework preserves HRT schedulability while improving QoS-aware deadline satisfaction, client-level service quality, and accelerator utilization compared to state-of-the-art scheduling approaches.

## 1. Introduction

Deep Neural Network (DNN) inference is increasingly used in safety-critical cyber-physical systems such as autonomous vehicles, robotics, industrial automation, and edge AI infrastructures [2,29]. These systems often execute mixed workloads on heterogeneous multi-accelerator platforms, where hard real-time (HRT) inference tasks coexist with soft real-time (SRT) requests generated by multiple clients [36,40,25].

Scheduling such workloads is challenging because DNN inference induces precedence-constrained execution graphs, accelerator heterogeneity couples allocation and priority decisions, and SRT requests arrive dynamically at runtime [30,22]. At the same time, HRT tasks must always satisfy their deadlines under worst-case execution assumptions.

Existing real-time scheduling approaches can provide timing guarantees, but are often based on static schedules that poorly adapt to runtime variability [20,12]. Conversely, adaptive online schedulers and multi-tenant inference systems improve utilization and responsiveness, but generally lack strict HRT guarantees [10,6]. Reinforcement Learning (RL) is attractive for online scheduling because it can learn workload-dependent policies without handcrafted heuristics [31], and this is the reason why it is employed in this work, but learned policies alone cannot be trusted for safety-critical scheduling.

This paper presents a scheduling framework for heterogeneous multi-accelerator systems that combines adaptive online scheduling with strict HRT guarantees. The framework separates optimization from safety. An RL-based online scheduler dynamically generates candidate schedule prefixes 1 by jointly selecting accelerator assignments and execution priorities, while a deterministic runtime safety layer validates each candidate before execution.

To enable runtime certification in heterogeneous precedence-constrained systems, we introduce a schedulability-preserving prefix replacement criterion based on machine-wise cuts, inspired by transfer schedulability [38]. The proposed criterion guarantees that runtime-generated schedule prefixes preserve the existence of a feasible continuation without requiring complete finish-time dominance with respect to a feasible reference schedule.

Experimental results on heterogeneous DNN accelerator platforms show that the proposed framework preserves HRT schedulability while improving SRT deadline satisfaction, client-level QoS, and accelerator utilization compared to state-of-the-art scheduling approaches.

The main contributions of this paper are:

-A formulation of mixed hard/soft real-time DNN inference scheduling on heterogeneous multi-accelerator systems using a DAG-based task model. -A schedulability-preserving runtime certification mechanism for heterogeneous precedence-constrained schedules. -An RL-based online scheduler for joint accelerator allocation and execution priority assignment within a deterministic HRT safety envelope. Our open-source implementation is available at: https://github.com/BlancoFrancescoGiulio/retnas. -Formal proofs showing that runtime certification preserves HRT schedulability independently of the employed policy (e.g., RL). Upper bound on runtime scheduling overhead -An experimental evaluation demonstrating improved SRT QoS and accelerator utilization while preserving HRT correctness.

## 2. System and Scheduling Model

We consider the problem of scheduling periodic hard real-time (HRT) tasks together with aperiodic soft real-time (SRT) jobs on a heterogeneous processing platform. The model follows standard real-time scheduling terminology and DAG-based task models [7,4,13,35,5].

### 2.1. Platform and DAG Job Model

The platform consists of a finite set of machines (processors or sub-accelerators), M = {1, . . . , M }. The platform is heterogeneous: the execution time of a computation depends on both the computation and the processor on which it executes. Thus, processors are modeled as unrelated machines.

Computations are represented as DAG jobs. A DAG job J is described by G J = (V J , E J ), where each vertex u ∈ V J is a sequential computation, called a sub-job, and each edge (u, v) ∈ E J is a precedence constraint. A sub-job u is called a terminal sub-job if it has no successors, i.e., ∄v ∈ V J : (u, v) ∈ E J . If sub-job u starts at time s u and finishes at time f u , then (u, v) ∈ E J implies s v ≥ f u . The completion time of job J is f J = max u∈V J f u . For each sub-job u, let M u ⊆ M denote the set of processors on which u is eligible to execute. For each eligible processor m ∈ M u , C u,m denotes the worst-case execution time (WCET) of u on m; for m / ∈ M u , we set C u,m = ∞. If u is assigned to processor m u , then under worst-case execution f u = s u + C u,mu .

Communication and data-movement assumptions. Prior real-time DNN scheduling approaches explicitly model fine-grained data-movement effects, including cross-processor communication, CPU/GPU data transfers, and layer-level pipelining or migration [24,16,39]. In this work, we incorporate data-movement costs into the profiled execution-time bounds C u,m . In particular, for HRT sub-jobs, C u,m conservatively accounts for the activation write-back and read costs required for the execution of sub-job u on accelerator m.

The runtime scheduler, instead, can exploit locality when two layers with a data dependency or two instances of the same layer execute sequentially on the same accelerator, by reusing locally available activations or weights. However, the HRT reference schedule construction and the runtime certification described in Sections 3.1 and 3.3 do not rely on such reuse. Indeed, after a runtime prefix replacement, another HRT or SRT sub-job may execute between two consecutive layers, or the successor may be assigned to a different accelerator, invalidating assumptions on local data availability.

### 2.2. Hard and Soft Real-Time Workloads

The HRT workload is a finite set of periodic DAG tasks T H = {τ 1 , . . . , τ n }. Each task is described by τ i = (G i , D i , T i ), where G i = (V i , E i ) is its DAG, D i is its relative deadline, and T i is its period. We consider constrained-deadline tasks, i.e., D i ≤ T i .

The k-th release of task τ i generates an HRT DAG job

J H i,k at time a i,k = a i,0 + kT i , with absolute deadline d H i,k = a i,k + D i . An HRT job meets its dead- line iff f J H i,k ≤ d H i,k .

The HRT requirement is that every generated job of every admitted HRT task meets its deadline.

The SRT workload consists of aperiodic DAG jobs arriving online. Each SRT job is modeled as J S j = (G j , a j , d S j , κ j ), where G j = (V j , E j ) is its DAG, a j is its arrival time, d S j is its soft absolute deadline, and κ j ∈ K identifies the client that generated the request. Unlike HRT tasks, SRT jobs are not known during admission control and no minimum inter-arrival time is assumed.

For SRT jobs, deadline misses do not compromise correctness, but degrade QoS. If f J S j > d S j , the request misses its soft deadline. SRT jobs may therefore be delayed, canceled, or rejected whenever necessary to preserve the HRT guarantee. For a set J S of requested SRT jobs, the system-level soft-deadline hit ratio is

H S = |{J S j ∈ J S | f J S j ≤ d S j }| |J S | .

The response time of an SRT job is R J S j = f J S j -a j .

### 2.3. Client-Level QoS and Fairness

The system-level hit ratio H S is an average system-level QoS metric and may hide severe client-level QoS degradation; as observed in real-time systems, averages offer no guarantees [1]. For example, one client may experience a high deadline-hit ratio while another receives very poor service, even though H S remains acceptable.

We therefore define client-level Service-Level Objective (SLO) achievement rates, which represent client-level QoS metrics. Let J S k = {J S j ∈ J S | κ j = k} be the set of SRT jobs requested by client k ∈ K. The SLO achievement rate of client k is

H S k = |{J S j ∈ J S k | f J S j ≤ d S j }| |J S k |

.

Each client may specify a target SLO achievement rate ĤS k . The scheduler should therefore improve not only the system-level SRT hit ratio, but also the clientlevel satisfaction with respect to these targets. A compact client-oriented QoS objective is to maximize

min k∈K H S k -ĤS k ,

subject to preserving all HRT deadlines. In cloud-oriented deployments, a client may correspond to a service tenant; in edge and cyber-physical deployments, a client may correspond to, e.g., a robot, device, sensor stream, or independent inference pipeline.

### 2.4. Scheduling Model and Objective

As in standard multiprocessor scheduling, scheduling consists of two coupled problems [13]: an allocation problem, which decides on which processor a computation executes, and a priority problem, which decides when and in what order ready computations execute. In system-architecture terminology, this corresponds to space-time scheduling: the spatial decision is processor assignment, while the temporal decision is execution ordering.

The scheduler operates at sub-job granularity and uses dynamic priorities to decide the execution ordering. The priority of a ready sub-job may change over time as a function of deadlines, laxities, processor availability, queue state, graph progress, and online scheduling decisions. HRT sub-jobs execute nonpreemptively. If an executing SRT sub-job must be interrupted to preserve HRT correctness, its partial execution is discarded and any later execution is treated as a new dispatch. We model this event as cancellation rather than preemption with resumption, which reflects accelerator architectures where saving and restoring the internal execution state may be unsupported or prohibitively expensive.

A schedule assigns each ready sub-job u to a processor m u and to an execution interval [s u , f u ). A schedule is valid if it satisfies: (i) eligibility, m u ∈ M u for every executed sub-job u; (ii) processor exclusivity, no processor executes more than one sub-job at any time; (iii) nonparallel execution, no sub-job executes on more than one processor at any time; and (iv) precedence preservation,

(u, v) ∈ E J implies s v ≥ f u .

The scheduling objective is lexicographic. First, all admitted HRT jobs must meet their deadlines under all admissible runtime executions. Second, subject to this hard constraint, the scheduler should improve SRT responsiveness, softdeadline hit ratios, client-level SLO satisfaction, and accelerator utilization by reducing idle intervals across heterogeneous accelerator resources.

## 3. Framework Overview

The proposed framework combines adaptive online scheduling with strict hard real-time (HRT) guarantees for heterogeneous multi-accelerator systems executing both HRT and soft real-time (SRT) DNN inference workloads.

The key idea is to separate safety from performance optimization. Runtime performance is optimized by an online scheduler, while HRT correctness is enforced independently through a deterministic runtime certification mechanism.

Figure 1 illustrates the overall framework. Offline, the system constructs a feasible HRT-only reference schedule under worst-case execution assumptions. At runtime, an online scheduling policy observes the current system state and generates candidate schedule prefixes that jointly schedule HRT and SRT subjobs. Before execution, each candidate prefix is validated by a runtime safety check. If the prefix is certified safe, it is executed; otherwise, execution falls back to the corresponding portion of the certified HRT reference schedule. As a consequence, HRT correctness does not depend on the online scheduler itself. The scheduler may improve SRT responsiveness, fairness, and accelerator utilization, but unsafe runtime decisions are filtered by the safety layer before execution.

Although this work employs a reinforcement learning (RL) scheduler, the framework itself is policy-agnostic. Any online scheduling policy capable of generating runtime candidate schedules can be enclosed within the same safety envelope.

### 3.1. Reference Schedule and Admission Control

Hard real-time correctness is derived from a feasible offline reference schedule constructed considering only the admitted HRT workload. During system initialization, the framework computes a WCET-based HRT-only schedule S ref over a finite analysis horizon. For periodic tasks, the natural horizon is the hyperperiod

H = lcm(T 1 , . . . , T n ).

For each HRT sub-job u, the reference schedule records its processor assignment and execution interval

If such a feasible schedule exists, the task is admitted and the newly generated schedule replaces the previous reference schedule. Otherwise, the task is rejected. A reference schedule is constructed offline, hence, its construction complexity does not affect runtime scheduling, and the framework is independent of the construction method. SRT clients are not admitted through the HRT feasibility test; a SRT client only registers its target SLO achievement rate. To improve the availability of contiguous slack regions, the reference schedule may optionally be transformed through a uniform forward shift of HRT executions while preserving all deadlines, so that at least one sub-job starts at its latest start time (LST).

### 3.2. Runtime Scheduling

The online scheduler is activated periodically with period T S . At activation time t k = kT S , the scheduler observes the current runtime state, including ready HRT and SRT sub-jobs, processor availability, queue occupancy, graph progress, and remaining execution tails inherited from previous scheduler windows.

In heterogeneous DNN inference systems, allocation and priority decisions [13] are tightly coupled because execution times depend on the selected accelerator.

Based on the observed runtime state, the scheduler generates a candidate schedule prefix for a bounded future interval. Unlike the offline reference schedule, the runtime scheduler is fully adaptive and may execute HRT sub-jobs earlier than in the reference schedule, dynamically reorder HRT and SRT work, select different eligible accelerators, leave processors temporarily idle, or opportunistically exploit runtime slack for SRT execution.

The scheduler activation period T S determines the tradeoff between runtime adaptability and scheduling overhead. Smaller activation periods improve responsiveness to newly arrived SRT jobs but increase runtime scheduler overhead.

Let τ sch denote an upper bound on the total runtime overhead including schedule generation, runtime certification, dispatching, and cancellation handling. This overhead is included in the certified timing model.

### 3.3. Runtime Safety Certification

The online scheduler is not trusted for HRT correctness. Consequently, every runtime-generated candidate prefix must be validated before execution.

A natural solution would be to directly test whether a candidate prefix preserves future HRT schedulability. However, exact runtime feasibility analysis is computationally impractical for heterogeneous DAG-based systems with precedence constraints and non-preemptive execution. Instead, the framework employs a bounded-time sufficient acceptance test derived from the offline reference schedule. Intuitively, the runtime certification mechanism verifies whether the candidate prefix preserves enough certified progress to guarantee that all unfinished HRT work can still complete feasibly after the checked cut.

If the candidate prefix is certified safe, execution proceeds according to the online schedule. Otherwise, execution falls back to the corresponding portion of the offline HRT reference schedule and all SRT work contained in the rejected prefix is discarded. The acceptance test therefore acts as a deterministic safety envelope surrounding the online scheduler. Unsafe runtime decisions may reduce performance, but they cannot compromise HRT correctness.

## 4. Runtime Safety Certification

This section formalizes the runtime certification mechanism used to guarantee hard real-time correctness independently of the online scheduler.

We first recall the intuition behind transfer schedulability on uniprocessors and explain why its classical scalar slack formulation does not directly extend to heterogeneous precedence-constrained systems. We then introduce a schedulability-preserving prefix replacement criterion suitable for heterogeneous multi-accelerator DNN scheduling.

### 4.1. From Transfer Schedulability to Prefix Certification

Transfer schedulability [38] provides a way to preserve schedulability by comparing an online execution with a feasible reference schedule. In its classical uniprocessor form, the online scheduler monitors whether the work that must match the reference schedule has become locally urgent. When a slackless interval is detected, the scheduler must give priority to the corresponding locally urgent work; otherwise, schedulability may no longer be transferable from the reference schedule.

We use this idea only as a design principle. We do not attempt to extend the uniprocessor slackless test directly to heterogeneous multiprocessor DAG scheduling. In our setting, feasibility depends jointly on machine assignment, non-preemptive execution, precedence constraints, and future resource contention across unrelated accelerators. Therefore, a scalar slack quantity is not sufficient to characterize whether the online state can still match a feasible continuation.

To see why, consider even the simpler case of a set J of independent, nonpreemptive, non-migratory jobs on M identical processors over an interval of length L. Let W = J∈J C J be the total remaining work, being C J the length of a job J. The condition W > M L proves infeasibility, but the converse is false: W ≤ M L does not imply that the jobs can be packed on the individual processors. For example, with two processors, L = 10, and three jobs of length C J = 6, the total work is W = 3C J = 18 < 20, but no feasible assignment exists. The corresponding decision problem contains P ||C max ≤ L as a special case, which is NP-hard by reduction from Partition.

The setting considered in this paper is strictly more general: processors are unrelated, since a sub-job u has execution time C u,m on machine m, and HRT jobs are DAGs whose vertices are non-preemptive sub-jobs. Consequently, the runtime safety layer deliberately uses a bounded-time sufficient test. If the test accepts, HRT schedulability is preserved; if it rejects, the system falls back to a certified continuation.

### 4.2. Schedulability-Preserving Prefix Replacement

Let J H denote the set of generated HRT DAG jobs under consideration, and let

V H = J∈J H V J

be the set of their HRT sub-jobs. Let S ref be a feasible WCET reference schedule for J H , and let S cand be a candidate runtime schedule. For a sub-job u, we write m S (u), s S (u), and f S (u) for its machine, start time, and finish time in schedule S.

### Definition 1 (Machine-wise cut). A machine-wise cut is a vector

T ⋆ = (t 1 , . . . , t M ),

where t m is a time instant referred to machine m. We refer to t m as the local cut time on machine m. Definition 2 (HRT Schedule Prefix). Given a schedule S and a machinewise cut T ⋆ , the HRT schedule prefix induced by T ⋆ is

P S (T ⋆ ) = u ∈ V H f S (u) ≤ t m S (u) .

That is, the prefix contains all HRT sub-jobs that complete no later than the local cut time of the machine to which they are assigned.

For a job J, we denote by

P J S (T ⋆ ) = P S (T ⋆ ) ∩ V J

the portion of its HRT sub-jobs belonging to the prefix.

### Definition 3 (Clean cut).

A machine-wise cut T ⋆ is clean for schedule S if no HRT sub-job crosses the cut on its assigned machine, i.e.,

∄u ∈ V H s.t. s S (u) < t m S (u) < f S (u).

SRT sub-jobs are not part of the HRT correctness argument; if an SRT sub-job crosses any certified cut, it is canceled at the cut and its partial execution is discarded.

### Definition 4 (Closed and open HRT jobs). An HRT job

J is closed in schedule S at cut T ⋆ if V J ⊆ P J S (T ⋆ ). Otherwise, J is open at T ⋆ .

Definition 5 (Open-interface sub-jobs). For a schedule S, an HRT sub-job u ∈ P J S (T ⋆ ) is an open-interface sub-job of job J if it has at least one successor of the same job outside the prefix:

∃v ∈ V J \ P J S (T ⋆ ) s.t. (u, v) ∈ E J .

We denote the set of such sub-jobs by I J S (T ⋆ ).

(u, v) ∈ E J , if v ∈ P J S cand (T ⋆ ), then u ∈ P J S cand (T ⋆ ) and f cand u ≤ s cand v . 2.

Completed terminal HRT sub-jobs are already safe. For every HRT job J, every terminal sub-job completed in the candidate prefix finishes no later than the absolute deadline of its job:

Theorem 1 (Schedulability-preserving prefix replacement). Let S ref be a feasible WCET reference schedule for the HRT jobs J H , and let S cand be a candidate schedule. Assume that T ⋆ is clean for both S ref and S cand . If S cand is a safe prefix replacement of S ref at T ⋆ , then replacing the HRT prefix of S ref with the HRT prefix of S cand preserves HRT schedulability.

Proof. Keep the candidate prefix and execute every remaining HRT sub-job in its reference interval on its reference machine, skipping all sub-jobs already completed in the candidate prefix.

By Condition 3, every remaining sub-job lies outside the reference prefix. Since T ⋆ is clean for S ref , its reference execution starts no earlier than the local cut on its reference machine. On each machine, candidate-prefix executions finish no later than the local cut, so they cannot overlap the retained reference executions. Together with Condition 1 and reference validity, this preserves eligibility, machine exclusivity, and non-parallel execution.

Precedence constraints within the candidate prefix hold by Condition 1, while those between remaining sub-jobs hold because their reference intervals are unchanged. For an edge (u, v) from the candidate prefix to a remaining sub-job, Condition 4 ensures

f cand u ≤ s ref v .

Conversely, an edge from a remaining sub-job into the candidate prefix is excluded by the precedence closure in Condition 1. Thus all precedence constraints are preserved.

Finally, consider any HRT job J. Every terminal sub-job in its candidate prefix meets d J by Condition 2, whether J is open or closed. Every remaining terminal sub-job meets d J because it executes in its feasible reference interval. Since every non-terminal vertex of a finite DAG has a path to a terminal vertex, precedence preservation implies that all sub-jobs of J finish by d J . Hence all HRT jobs meet their deadlines.

## 5. Online Scheduler

This section describes how the proposed framework applies the schedulabilitypreserving prefix replacement theory of Section 4 at runtime.

The framework employs an online scheduler that periodically generates candidate schedule prefixes for the currently available HRT and SRT sub-jobs. The scheduler itself is not trusted for hard real-time correctness. Consequently, every runtime-generated candidate prefix is validated through the runtime certification mechanism derived from Theorem 1 before execution.

If the candidate prefix satisfies the certification conditions, it is committed for execution. Otherwise, the system falls back to the corresponding portion of the certified HRT reference schedule and any SRT work contained in the rejected prefix is discarded.

Although the framework is scheduler-agnostic, this work employs a reinforcement learning (RL)-based scheduler that jointly optimizes accelerator assignment and execution ordering to improve SRT QoS and accelerator utilization while remaining enclosed within the deterministic HRT safety envelope.

Section 5.1 describes the runtime HRT certification mechanism, Section 5.2 briefly reviews the RL background used in this work, and Section 5.3 presents the proposed RL scheduler policy.

### 5.2. Background on Reinforcement Learning

RL is a machine learning paradigm in which an agent learns a decision policy through repeated interaction with an environment. At each decision step t, the agent observes the current state s t , selects an action a t according to a policy π(a t |s t ), and receives a scalar reward r t reflecting the quality of the selected action. The environment then transitions to a new state s t+1 [34].

The objective of RL is to learn a policy maximizing the expected discounted cumulative reward:

J(π) = E π [ ∞ t=0 η t r t ]

, where η ∈ [0, 1) is the reward discount factor.

In actor-critic methods, the policy is represented by an actor network, while a separate critic network estimates the expected long-term utility of actions. More specifically, the critic approximates the action-value function

Q π (s, a) = E π ∞ k=0 η k r t+k s t = s, a t = a .

This work employs Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG) [23], an offpolicy actor-critic algorithm designed for continuous action spaces. DDPG represents both actor and critic through deep neural networks and updates them during the training phase, using transitions collected during runtime interacwith the environment. Training samples are stored in a replay buffer as tuples (s t , a t , r t , s t+1 ), which are later sampled uniformly to reduce temporal correlation and improve training stability. The critic is trained to minimize the Bellman error:

L(ϕ) = E (Q ϕ (s t , a t ) -y t )

2 , where the target value is

y t = r t + ηQ ϕ -(s t+1 , π θ -(s t+1

)) , and ϕ -and θ -denote slowly updated targetnetwork parameters. The actor parameters θ are updated through deterministic policy gradients:

∇ θ J(θ) = E ∇ a Q ϕ (s, a) a=π θ (s) ∇ θ π θ (s) .

After the training phase, the critic is no longer used during deployment, and the actor alone serves as the scheduling policy. DDPG is particularly suitable for scheduling problems because it naturally supports high-dimensional continuous action spaces, allowing the policy to jointly encode scheduling priorities and accelerator selection decisions.

### 5.3. RL-based Scheduler Policy

The online scheduler considered in this work is based on the DDPG [23] algorithm described in Section 5.2. We considered alternative RL formulations, including discrete-action methods such as Q-learning. However, directly representing the joint sub-job ordering and accelerator-assignment decisions as discrete actions would result in a combinatorial action space that grows rapidly with the number of ready sub-jobs and available accelerators. Even other formulations, such as multi-armed bandit formulations [19,26], are also not well suited to this problem because scheduling decisions are inherently sequential and affect future system states; capturing such state transitions and delayed consequences therefore requires a sequential decision-making formulation. We adopt DDPG and encode the scheduling decisions through continuous priority values, which are subsequently decoded execution priorities and accelerator assignments. Importantly, priorities are an internal mechanism used by the online policy to encode and construct a candidate schedule. Once the candidate schedule has been generated, its validity and certification depend only on the resulting machine assignments, execution intervals, precedence constraints, and not on the priority values that produced it. Therefore, priorities do not appear in Definition 6.

We consider the state as the ready sub-jobs and the current accelerator occupancy. At each scheduler activation, the policy observes the current runtime state and generates a candidate schedule by jointly selecting sub-job priorities and accelerator assignments. This formulation allows the scheduler to adapt to time-varying workload arrivals and heterogeneous accelerator availability while accounting for precedence constraints, resource contention, and execution tails. The input sequence is prepended with a vector encoding the residual occupancy of each accelerator: an idle accelerator is represented by zero (e.g., ∆M = 0), whereas a busy accelerator is represented by its remaining busy time (e.g., ∆1 > 0). Each subsequent element encodes a ready HRT or SRT sub-job. For each input element, the policy outputs a temporal priority and M accelerator-selection scores. The first output, corresponding to the acceleratoroccupancy state vector rather than to a sub-job, is discarded.

As shown in Figure 4, the runtime state is encoded as a sequence of fixedlength feature vectors, one for each ready sub-job in ready queue RQ t , naturally supporting a variable number of schedulable sub-jobs. The feature representation includes model and layer identifiers, relative deadlines, waiting times, profiled execution latencies for each accelerator, the identifier of the client generating the request, the current client-level deadline-hit ratio H S k , the corresponding target SLO achievement rate ĤS k , and two boolean flags identifying HRT and QoS-aware sub-jobs. Each feature vector therefore has length 9 + M , where M is the number of accelerators. The input sequence is preceded by an additional 9 + M -dimensional all-zero vector except M entries, which show the residual busy time of each accelerator inherited from previously dispatched execution tails, allowing the policy to reason about near-future accelerator availability and congestion. Ready sub-jobs are then provided in deadline order.

For each ready sub-job, the actor network emits an action encoded as an (M + 1)-dimensional vector, as follows: (i) a scalar temporal priority and (ii) an M -dimensional spatial priority vector over the accelerators. The maximum spatial priority among the eligible accelerators in M v determines the selected accelerator.

The continuous policy outputs are decoded into a discrete schedule by dispatching, whenever an accelerator becomes available, the highest-priority eligible sub-job onto the accelerator selected by the spatial priority vector.

Both actor and critic are implemented as recurrent neural networks to support variable-sized ready queues. In our implementation, we employ GRU layers for improved computational efficiency. The recurrent encoder captures sequencelevel scheduling context such as deadline structure, accelerator contention, and queue composition. The critic estimates the action-value function Q ϕ (s, a) from the recurrent hidden state.

Training follows standard off-policy DDPG methodology. Runtime transitions (s t , a t , r t , s t+1 ) are stored in a replay buffer and sampled uniformly to reduce temporal correlation. Target networks and soft parameter updates are used to stabilize training [23]. Exploration noise is applied only during training.

We designed a reward function to prioritize deadline satisfaction and, among feasible outcomes, improve client-level QoS balance and accelerator utilization. The relative importance of the different reward components is controlled by tunable parameters, which are set before training and can be selected empirically, e.g., through grid search, according to the desired trade-off among the optimization objectives. The reward is decomposed into four components:

r t = R deadline t + R slack t + R QoS t + R HRT t .(1)

The first three reward components, i.e., R deadline t , R slack t , and R QoS t , pertain to the SRT workload, whereas R HRT t pertains to the HRT workload. The deadline satisfaction component, which penalizes deadline misses, is:

R deadline t = l∈RQ SRT t ∆ t,l A t,l ,(2)

where

A t,l = α, f l ≤ d l -β, otherwise. (3

)

where α and β are tunable reward hyperparameters.

The normalized completion-time improvement component, which favors earlier completions, is:

R slack t = γ l∈RQ SRT t ∆ t,l d l -f l d l -a l . (4

)

The client-level QoS balancing component, which encourages client-level SLO achievement rates to remain close to their target values, is:

R QoS t = -λ l∈RQ SRT t ∆ t,l H S κ l -ĤS κ l ,(5)

where H S κ l is the current deadline-hit ratio of the client generating sub-job l, and ĤS κ l is the corresponding target SLO achievement rate defined in Section 2.3; λ is a tunable reward hyperparameter.

Finally, HRT deadline misses are strongly penalized through:

R HRT t = -µN HRT miss (t),(6)

where N HRT miss (t) is the number of HRT deadline misses observed during the scheduling window and µ ≫ 1 is a large penalty coefficient.

The over-the-horizon weighting factor is:

∆ t,l = 1, f l < t + T S δ, otherwise.(7)

During training, any HRT deadline miss produces a catastrophic negative reward through R HRT t . Unlike for the SRT workload, where R slack t encourages earlier completion, the HRT reward does not incentivize completion earlier than necessary. During deployment, exploration is disabled and candidate schedules are executed only if accepted by the schedulability-preserving runtime certification mechanism described in Section 4. Consequently, the RL policy affects only QoS-aware optimization and candidate schedule quality, while HRT correctness is entirely enforced by the deterministic safety layer. Regarding the reference schedule construction, in the experiments we have employed several techniques. The main two are: EDF ordering with earliest-completion accelerator selection, and a recurrent heuristic inspired by [6] favouring larger deadline slack. Both methods are followed by deterministic validation of precedences and HRT deadlines.

6 Experimental Results

### 6.1. Experimental Setup

We evaluate the proposed framework on a heterogeneous multi-accelerator platform with six DNN accelerators, following the established heterogeneous setup of [6,31], enabling the comparison with prior scheduling techniques. In architectural terms, three accelerators implement a row-stationary dataflow and an organization close to Eyeriss [9]; the other three implement a weight-stationary dataflow and an organization close to Simba [32]. For each accelerator family, two instances are small and one instance is large. Hence, the platform exposes both dataflow heterogeneity and size heterogeneity, and sub-job execution times depend on the selected accelerator. We consider representative DNN inference workloads from image classification and object detection. The image-classification set includes AlexNet, InceptionV3, and ResNet50, while the object-detection set includes YOLO-v2. Each inference request is represented as a DAG of layer-level sub-jobs, with precedence constraints induced by the network topology. For every layer and eligible accelerator, we use profiled execution latencies as the scheduling costs. SRT arrivals follow a Google-like workload model derived from large-scale production traces. Following the workload generator of Da Costa et al. [11], inter-arrival times are sampled from a heavy-tailed Pareto process. Let d denote the target mean inter-arrival time, corresponding to the generator's dynamism parameter. For each submitted SRT job i, we sample

X i ∼ Pareto(α = 4, x m = 1) and set a i = a i-1 +3d(X i -1). Since E[X i -1] = 1/3, the expected inter-arrival time is E[a i -a i-1 ] = d.

We evaluate two workload intensities: d = 40 ms, denoting a moderate-pressure workload, and d = 10 ms, denoting a high-pressure workload with four times more frequent SRT arrivals.

Each SRT request is associated with a client k ∈ K. For each client, we measure the client-level SLO achievement rate H S k , defined as the fraction of that client's SRT jobs that complete by their soft deadline. Client targets are chosen from ĤS k ∈ {70%, 80%, 90%}. The aggregate SRT hit ratio H S measures system-level SRT timeliness, while the quantity min k∈K H S k -ĤS k captures the worst client-level SLO margin. If this value is non-negative, all clients meet their target SLO achievement rate; negative values indicate that at least one client is below its target. Therefore, our secondary objective, after preserving HRT schedulability, is to improve H S while avoiding negative client-level SLO margins for each client. Across all certified experiments, no admitted HRT job misses its deadline; this only validates the implementation, whereas the HRT guarantee follows from Theorem 1. The measurement simply confirms that the implementation follows the certified execution protocol. Regarding the reward parameters, we employed α = 0.01 for SRT deadline hits, β = 0.11 for SRT misses, µ = 30 for HRT misses, λ = 0.1 for client-level QoS imbalance, and δ = 0.05 for outcomes beyond the current scheduling window. These coefficients were selected through a grid search over training simulations. For example, µ was chosen so that HRT misses make the total reward negative despite the other components, while the remaining terms were balanced to prevent any secondary objective from dominating.

### 6.2. Baselines

We compare against representative online DNN schedulers, slack-based real-time mechanisms, and simple dispatch heuristics, all implemented at layer granularity with the same profiled accelerator latencies. Methods that do not natively provide HRT guarantees in our heterogeneous DAG-based model are evaluated as HRT-safe wrapped variants, obtained by enclosing them in the same prefixreplacement safety envelope used by our framework. This also shows that the proposed certification-and-fallback mechanism is not specific to our RL scheduler. Any online scheduling policy can be enclosed within the same safety envelope: if the policy is effective at scheduling real-time jobs on heterogeneous platforms, the fallback mechanism can be used to make its execution HRT-safe. We include PREMA [10], adapting its priority/allocation logic to our heterogeneous layer-level simulator. In particular, our adaptation selects among the highest-aging ready layers according to their expected latency, and assigns them to accelerators considering current availability. We also include a client-aware RL policy [31] that augments the policy state and reward with client-level SLO information based on the deadline-hit rate SLI. We also employ the algorithms proposed by Tang et al. [35] by constructing an HRT schedule with H-EDF or FF, deriving per-accelerator slack windows, and admitting SRT layers only when they can be placed by RC, TC, or TCSD without violating release times, latest-start-time bounds, machine order, or DNN precedence constraints. We implement SSML [15], originally formulated for a uniprocessor setting, as a conservative scheduler-core adaptation that computes a modified-laEDF slack budget σ from the remaining HRT layer workload and admits a non-preemptive SRT layer only if its whole execution fits both the available slack budget and the current scheduling window. We adapt CD-MSA [37] by applying its EDF ordering, k i=1 C i ≤ D k schedulability test, and laxity-aware promotion rule at the DNNrequest level, while estimating remaining execution costs from our heterogeneous layer latencies and subsequently mapping the selected layers to accelerators. We also include SJF, SSJF, FCFS, and LCFS as simple online dispatch heuristics for ready DNN layers, augmented with a heuristic to select, for each sub-job, the accelerator expected to yield the shortest completion time based on dataflow affinity and current resource utilization.

Scheduler overhead. We account for the end-to-end runtime overhead incurred at each scheduling instant, including state encoding, policy inference, runtime certification, and dispatching/cancellation bookkeeping. We denote this overhead by τ sch = τ enc + τ policy + τ cert + τ disp . This term is treated as the worst-case execution time of the online scheduler and is charged at every scheduling activation in the experimental timing model. The scheduler is activated every T S = 5 ms.

To make τ sch bounded, the number of sub-jobs visible to the online policy is limited to a fixed maximum N max : all ready HRT sub-jobs are always included, while the SRT ready queue is truncated when necessary; more priority is given to the SRT jobs pertaining to clients with a SLO achievement rate lower than the target. This bounds the cost of both state encoding and policy inference independently of the total SRT backlog. For the GRU policy with hidden size 192, the measured cost is 592 cycles per encoded sub-job on the smallest accelerator. With N max = 64, this gives τ policy = 64 • 592 = 37888 cycles = 37.9 µs at 1 GHz, i.e., 0.76% of the scheduling period. The runtime certification step is also bounded: it scans at most all the N HRT sub-jobs, therefore it has O(N ) complexity and is bounded by N max . Moreover, since sub-jobs are distributed across scheduling periods, N is only an upper bound. Increasing the number of HRT tasks or DAG length increases N linearly.

Scheduling period analysis. We evaluate the impact of T S under high pressure (d = 10 ms) through two experiments: (i) deploying a policy trained with T S = 5 ms at different scheduling periods, and (ii) training and testing a separate policy for each period. For T S ∈ {0.5, 1, 1.25, 2.5, 5, 10} ms, the policy trained at 5 ms achieves SRT hit ratios H S of 73.73%, 76.84%, 69.45%, 74.96%, 81.40%, and 71.12%, respectively. Although performance peaks at the training period, the policy retains effectiveness across the tested periods, demonstrating transfer across scheduling frequencies. Conversely, policies trained specifically at 0.5, 1, 1.25, 2.5, and 10 ms achieve only 25.69%, 27.92%, 46.82%, 48.79%, and 62.81%, respectively. We attribute the poorer results of training with short periods to the following reasons. Shorter windows contain fewer completed sub-jobs contributing to the reward and provide less execution time over which the policy can exploit accelerator availability. Moreover, scheduling overhead accounts for a larger fraction of execution time. Conversely, large periods are constrained by timing requirements of both HRT and SRT jobs. Their relative deadlines must accommodate waiting time, scheduling overhead, and the required execution time. Additional timing slack also leaves more room for online optimization.

Hard-periodic/soft-aperiodic scheduling on heterogeneous resources. Tang et al. [35] study the joint scheduling of hard periodic and soft aperiodic tasks on heterogeneous resources. Their approach is divided into two phases: an offline phase, where hard periodic tasks are scheduled using either Heterogeneous EDF (H-EDF) or Fastest First (FF); then, online, soft aperiodic tasks are scheduled using the per-resource slack windows extracted from the resulting hard schedule, using Resource-Centric (RC), Task-Centric (TC), or Task-Centric with Slack Defragmentation (TCSD). Static solutions such as LayerWeaver [27] and AI-MT [3] assign DNN layers to accelerators based on analytical models, but they cannot adapt to runtime variability. Slack stealing and modified look-ahead EDF. Slack stealing is a classical mechanism for improving the responsiveness of soft aperiodic work in systems where hard periodic tasks have already been guaranteed. A slack stealer exploits unused processing time from the periodic schedule to execute the aperiodic work, while ensuring that previously guaranteed periodic deadlines are not violated [21]. This idea is closely related to the look-ahead EDF (laEDF) mechanisms used in real-time dynamic voltage scaling: Pillai and Shin [28] compute the minimum amount of periodic work that must execute before the next deadline, deferring the remaining work to later intervals when this is safe. SSML [15] adapts laEDF principle to hard-periodic and soft-aperiodic scheduling for uniprocessor systems with independent tasks.

## 8. Conclusion

This paper presented a scheduling framework for heterogeneous multi-accelerator systems executing both hard real-time and soft real-time QoS-aware DNN inference workloads. The proposed approach combines an online RL-based sched-uler with a deterministic runtime certification mechanism that guarantees HRT correctness independently of the learned policy. To enable safe runtime adaptation, we introduced a schedulability-preserving prefix replacement criterion for heterogeneous precedence-constrained schedules. Experimental results showed that the framework preserves HRT schedulability while improving QoS-aware deadline satisfaction, client-level service quality, and accelerator utilization compared to state-of-the-art scheduling approaches. Future work includes extending the framework to support additional runtime uncertainties, workload prediction mechanisms, and alternative QoS optimization objectives.
